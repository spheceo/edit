# Compilation writing guide

A compilation is several cases, one per video, under one title. Inside a case
the Cut-In (Canto) framework applies as it is: the case opening, Anchor →
Bridge → Teaser, the outro, the tense, the restraint and the words to handle
with care. This guide adds what a compilation needs around the cases: the
opening, the transitions, each case's intro clips, and the title that holds
them together. Where the two guides differ, follow this one: the Cut-In
guide's two-split intro, its strict sequence and its single conclusion on the
final cut-in describe a script with one case, and here every case has its own
opening and its own outro.

read_script names the purpose of every cut-in. Write each to its purpose.

## The title decides everything

The viewer clicked because of the title and the thumbnail. Give them what they
came for at the start, and keep pointing back to it. Run every sentence through
three filters before it is saved:

1. **Does it match the title?** If the title is "When Cops Discover Horrifying
   Crime Scenes", the viewer came for the discovery. Long legal explanations,
   random background and side conversations that do not lead to the discovery
   pull away from it.
2. **Does it add value?** New context, more tension, or help understanding what
   is on screen. If it does none of these, the sentence is not needed.
3. **Does it feel natural?** Write as if explaining the case to a friend:
   clear sentences, plain words, a human touch. No fiction-style drama, no
   complex vocabulary, no heavy sentences.

Ask of every decision: does this keep the viewer curious about what happens
next? Think of yourself less as a storyteller and more as a curator of the
important moments.

## A standard compilation: the title opens with "When"

In order:

1. Intro clips
2. **The hook**
3. More intro clips
4. **The title line**
5. For each case: **the transition**, that case's intro clips, **the case
   opening**, then footage and cut-ins through to **the case's outro**

### The intro clips

The first clip must connect with the title. Take the title apart into its
keywords, then choose a moment that shows them. For "When Cops Discover Kids
Living in Deplorable Conditions" the keywords are cops, kids and deplorable
conditions, and the clip chosen was an officer asking "Did you put a human in
that playpen?": a cop appears, the child is the centre of the discussion, and
the condition is plainly deplorable. It matches the title. Never open on
something far from that context.

### The hook

Simple and concise, and holding everything the title says. Usually two
sentences: a general statement of the theme, then the question the video
answers, most often "But what happens when…?".

- "Some parents are capable of the unthinkable – letting their children live in
  deplorable conditions. But what happens when officers arrive and uncover a
  house of horror?" It speaks of children living in deplorable conditions, of
  cops finding them, and nothing else.
- "Some disturbed cops believe they can escape justice after committing
  horrific crimes. But what happens when their plan falls apart, and the truth
  catches up to them?"
- "An officer’s duty requires responding to every kind of call — from a simple
  traffic stop to a complex investigation. But what happens when routine calls
  escalate, and officers find themselves facing the most horrifying discoveries
  of their careers?"
- "Many welfare checks are routine and end without incident - but what happens
  when officers uncover children trapped inside their parents’ house of
  horror?"

The hook names no person, date or place. Those belong to the cases.

The hook and the title line follow the title's meaning, not every one of its
words: where the title says "Predator", write around it as the Cut-In guide's
words to handle with care describe ("deeply disturbing officers", not
"predator cops").

### The title line

One sentence that turns the title into what the viewer is about to watch:

- "These were the moments when killer cops realized they’d been caught
  red-handed."
- "These were the moments when good cops went pale after horrifying
  discoveries…"
- "These were the moments when good cops discovered horrifying crime scenes…"

### The transitions

A transition resets the viewer's attention. It is one sentence, it ends on
"…", and the case's intro clips follow it.

**The first transition** always begins "We begin with the case of…", "We begin
with a case where…" or "Starting with the case where…". It is what tells the
viewer the title's promise is being kept, so it must point straight at the
title. For "When Parents Abandon Their Children":

- Right: "We begin with a case in which a young girl was discovered locked by
  her parents in a nasty shed for more than 24 hours…" ("locked by her parents"
  is the abandoning; "a young girl" is the child.)
- Not this: "We begin with the case of a man who found a girl locked inside a
  shelter…", "…a girl who spent 24h locked inside a shelter…", "…an officer who
  made a shocking discovery inside a shelter…". All of it happened, but none of
  it says what the title promised.

**Later transitions** re-ignite curiosity. Name the theme again, tease what is
particular to this case, and lead into its intro clips. "Next is another case."
does none of that. Say what these people did or went through, specifically; a
general line that could introduce any case reads as written by a machine.

- "Moving on to the next case where a mother was reckless enough to abandon her
  own child on the road, believing that twisting her story would save her from
  a harsh fate…"
- "Moving on to the next case where a sergeant believed that acting insane
  would make him appear less guilty of taking his own girlfriend’s life…"
- "Up next, we have a case where what began as a welfare check at a residence
  escalated into a disgusting discovery, unraveling a true house of horrors…"
- "Up next, we have a case where a routine welfare check on a vehicle escalated
  into one of the most gruesome discoveries officers would ever make in their
  careers…"
- "From one horrifying discovery to another, in the next case, officers were
  called by hunters after a find that made even the toughest of them uneasy…"
- "That case was already disturbing, but the next one went even further, when a
  former officer began communicating with a 16-year-old girl… only to take her
  life in the end…"
- "Moving on to the case where officers confronted a couple more focused on
  getting their next hit than caring for their little one, who was left
  freezing in the cold New Mexico winter…"

Vary how they open across the script. Do not give away the outcome.

Every transition, including those into reused cases, must connect the case to
the video's title. Name the theme and the specific, sourced event that delivers
it; announcing a person or place alone does not fulfil the title's promise.
Introduce the last case as the next case too: "Up next…" or "Moving on to the
next case…". Never call it "the final case", "the last case" or "our final
case", or otherwise signal that the video is about to end.

### The case's intro clips

The clips after a transition show what it just said. They are promises: the
viewer should feel "I’m already getting what I came for". If a clip comes from
ten minutes into the footage and the viewer waits ten minutes to reach it,
they leave. Trim what comes before it, bridge the gap with narration, or pick
a moment that arrives sooner.

### The case opening

"On [date], …". Its first words are always the date. Three parts:

- **The date**, as that video or its research gives it: the day of the call,
  the tip, or whatever first set the case in motion. Do not open on the tip,
  the agency or the person and leave the date for later, and do not drop it
  because the case began with a tip rather than a call. Look through the
  research before deciding there is none; only then, never making one up,
  open on the place and how the call came in.
- **The problem**: who called, about what, and where.
- **A teaser** for what the first footage shows.

"On June 20, 2025, officers in New Mexico received a call reporting that a
child had been locked in a shed, with no sign of her parents nearby. Upon
arriving, what they found revealed a level of mistreatment so severe it would
leave anyone speechless…"

When the footage opens in the middle of the action, with officers already
chasing someone, write this paragraph with more detail so the viewer
understands what is happening, and let the first clip run longer. Sometimes the
opening is too plain to tease; most of the time it is not.

### The cut-ins inside a case

The structure is the Cut-In one, in this guide's words:

1. **Anchor**: what just happened. "At this point, officers began noticing
   inconsistencies in the mother’s story…"
2. **Impact**: why it matters. "…raising serious concerns about what was really
   happening inside the home."
3. **Teaser**, optional: what is coming. "Moments later, the situation would
   escalate even further."

Not every cut-in needs all three. Wrap up what the viewer has just seen, then
build a soft bridge into what comes next. Whatever a cut-in teases must be the
first thing the next clip shows: if it says officers spoke to the boy, the boy
is on screen when the footage returns.

Ways a cut-in can begin, to adapt rather than repeat:

- "At this point…"
- "Inside the residence…"
- "As the officers entered the home…"
- "According to the witness…"
- "Speaking with the witness, it became clear that…"
- "Hearing the child say that…"
- "With the child now safe and secured…"
- "The mother, later identified as…"
- "The conditions inside the home were truly unsettling…"
- "As officers continued questioning…"
- "What initially seemed like a routine call quickly took a darker turn…"

### How not to summarise

Summarising is the commonest mistake. The viewer is not reading the script;
they are watching the video. If the cut-in tells them what the footage just
showed, they get the same information twice, and across a whole script that is
boring.

The problem is never saying what happened, it is how. A summary: "The officer
approached the residence, asking if someone was inside. Then, a girl answered,
and she said she was alone and her parents were in town." The same moment,
narrated: "Inside the residence, officers found a 6-year-old girl locked inside
a shed by her parents for more than 24 hours. At that point, there were no
adults present, and the incident had been reported by a man who alleged that a
child with visible bruises had been found trapped in the home. While the
reasons for leaving her there remained unclear, the very act was deeply
unsettling."

**Before writing a case**, read the whole video and that case's research, and
note what is useful: names, ages, what records show, what happened before and
after the footage. Note what the video's own voice-over says too, so the
narration neither repeats it nor copies its wording. Sharing a fact with it now
and then is normal; doing it in every cut-in is not. With notes there is
always something worth saying, and the viewer feels the case is understood.

**When a cut-in will not come**, write two sentences only, from any two of:

1. what just happened,
2. what will happen,
3. an analysis of the situation,
4. something the viewer needs to know, usually from the notes.

- What just happened + what will happen: "The mother tried to portray herself
  as innocent, believing she could fool the officers. But what she didn’t know
  yet was that the officers were about to bring evidence that would shatter her
  performance completely…"
- What just happened + an important piece of information: "The man claimed he
  hadn’t seen his girlfriend in the past few days, but the officers knew his
  story was full of lies. Surveillance footage from a residence next to the
  woman’s house showed him repeatedly going there during the days she was
  missing — the same house where her body was later found."
- An analysis + what will happen: "The house was in complete disarray, with
  trash scattered everywhere and expired food left out on the tables. Still,
  the officers kept digging deeper, trying to shed some light on what had
  happened to the girl…"

A cut-in can also compress footage that was left out. Instead of five minutes
of repeated questioning, the strongest moments are shown and the cut-in carries
the rest.

### The outro of each case

Every case ends with closure: the charges filed, the sentence, or where the
investigation stands. "In the end, [Name] was charged with…", "As the
investigation continued, detectives later discovered…". Keep it short. It is a
wrap-up, not a full explanation, and it carries no tease. Use only the outcome
that case's research supports; if none became public, end on the last verified
step, stated plainly.

End on the decisive step and stop there. Leave out what came after it when it
leaves the case hanging or reopens it: how a resignation was timed, that no
charges were ever filed, a later job, where the person is now. Those lines
undo the finality the outro is for.

- Ending on "In the end, Chaisson agreed to take the lie detector test on the
  condition that the city would not prosecute him. According to records 5
  Investigates obtained after a months-long public records battle, he failed
  the test, admitted to the allegations and resigned, leaving the department on
  November 22, 2013." is right. Going on with "…and using vacation time to push
  his resignation date to January 6. No charges were ever filed, and he went on
  to land a top job with the Massachusetts Department of Public Health, where
  he was no longer working once the story became public." leaves it hanging.

- "In the end, Sarah was charged with neglect and violation of probation."
- "In the end, William pleaded guilty to murder and violating his oath. He was
  sentenced to a minimum of 30 years in prison."
- "In the end, Leroy was charged with an open count of first-degree murder and
  tampering with evidence in connection with his mother’s death."

## A hidden compilation: the title reads like one case

The first case is written exactly as a single-case video, by the Cut-In
framework: the intro paragraph, the case opening, the story, the outro. The
title, the intro and the hook are about that case alone. Nothing before its
outro says or hints that another case follows, so there is no general hook, no
"These were the moments…" and no "We begin with…".

Straight after the first case's outro comes the first transition, "Moving on
to the case…", and from there every case is built as in a standard
compilation: transition, intro clips, case opening, footage and cut-ins, outro.
The viewer learns it is a compilation only at that first transition, so make
it specific and worth staying for.

## Reuse cases

A case marked "REUSE" with a link is one the channel has already covered in an
earlier video. Its finished segment is lifted from that video by the editor
and dropped into this one, which saves writing, editing and subtitling it
again.

For a reuse case, write only:

- a strong transition into it that fits the title and theme of this video,
- with the link saved on that transition as a comment (set_reuse_link), never
  written in the narration.

No intro clips, no case opening, no cut-ins and no outro: the finished segment
already has them. Think of it as a connecting piece, there so the viewer
experiences one seamless video. You cannot watch the linked video, so take what
the case is about from its title and from the producer, and ask for a line
about it when the title alone does not say what happened. Never guess its
facts.

In a hidden compilation whose first case is the reuse, the script begins with
a cut-in that carries the reuse link alone, with no narration written on it.
The cut-in after it is the transition into the second case.

## Tone and language

- Past tense throughout the narration. The standard compilation's hook is the
  exception.
- Grounded and factual. Let the footage carry the emotional weight.
- Never guess a motive, and never write what does not happen in the case or
  what is not obvious from it.
- No over-dramatic wording and nothing like a crime novel: this is narrating
  reality, not writing a film.
- Plain language, as if speaking to an ordinary person who is simply interested
  in the video. Complex language pushes them away.
- Keep each person's name and gender the same from their first mention to the
  outro.
- When a cut-in can be shorter and more interesting, make it so.

## Common mistakes

- Summarising footage the viewer has just watched.
- Assuming something that is not clearly shown.
- A complicated word where a simple one would do.
- A long intro that does not connect to the title.
- A cut-in forced in where silence would be stronger.
- Repeating in each cut-in what the video's own narrator said.
- A transition so general it could introduce any case.

## Before proposing the script

- Does the intro clearly match the title?
- Do the first three to five minutes hold back what the footage is about to
  reveal (the Cut-In guide's tension in the first minutes)?
- Do the intro clips match what is being said around them?
- Does each transition connect its case to the title and build curiosity?
- Is the last case introduced as the next case, without announcing the end?
- Is every cut-in natural, and does it add something?
- Is anything repetitive or unnecessary?
- Does the pacing feel smooth?

## Reference: one case, start to finish

From "When Parents Abandon Their Children", narration only, with what each
piece is. The people, dates and outcomes belong to that case; never borrow
them.

1. Transition: "We begin with a case in which a young girl was discovered
   locked by her parents in a nasty shed for more than 24 hours…" (then four
   intro clips of 2-7 seconds).
2. Case opening: "On June 20, 2025, officers in New Mexico received a call
   reporting that a child had been locked in a shed, with no sign of her
   parents nearby. Upon arriving, what they found revealed a level of
   mistreatment so severe it would leave anyone speechless…" (then a 43-second
   clip).
3. "Inside the residence, officers found a 6-year-old girl locked inside a shed
   by her parents for more than 24 hours. At that point, there were no adults
   present, and the incident had been reported by a man who alleged that a
   child with visible bruises had been found trapped in the home. While the
   reasons for leaving her there remained unclear, the very act was deeply
   unsettling."
4. "The conditions the girl was subjected to were truly disturbing. Being left
   in nearly 100-degree weather with no water or any way to call for help was
   extremely concerning and highlighted the severe mistreatment she endured."
5. "Hearing the girl say that she was left alone by her parents “only when she
   was bad” would break anyone’s heart. What might have been going through her
   parents’ minds for them to leave her there is unknown, but it was clear that
   the girl was in danger. She had been left for hours with no access to food,
   water, or her parents. Rushing to secure her safety, officers tried to find
   a way to reach her, but no doors or keys were accessible, leaving them with
   no choice but to force entry."
6. "At this point, with the girl safe in the officers’ care, deputies began
   inspecting the shed, but the conditions they uncovered only deepened their
   concern about the situation…"
7. "The conditions of the shed were truly unsettling. The sheer amount of dirt,
   the absence of food and water, and the sleeping conditions painted a clear
   picture of what the girl had endured during those hours. When paramedics
   examined the girl, they found bruises on her body, and other injuries
   suggested she had hurt herself from falls. Nearly two hours after the girl
   was discovered, her mother, later identified as 33-year-old Samantha Angli,
   finally arrived, acting as if completely unaware of the crime she had
   committed."
8. "Questioning the officers about what was happening was an astonishing
   display of audacity. Acting as if she didn’t know her daughter had been left
   inside a shed for hours revealed a deeply troubled mindset. Not only would
   the mother prove to be reckless, but the stepfather, later identified as
   56-year-old Bruce Anglin, who arrived shortly after, would also demonstrate
   his negligence…"
9. "Bruce’s claims that no one would take his daughter and that he would sue
   the officers were deeply irritating. What he thought might happen to them
   after leaving the girl in such conditions was unclear, but it was obvious he
   was trying to escape responsibility by playing the protective father. There
   was, however, no room for excuses, and both parents would soon face the
   legal consequences of their mistreatment."
10. Outro: "Later in the investigation, Bruce admitted that he had locked the
    girl in the shed as a form of punishment, while Samantha claimed she had
    tried to stop him, but clearly failed. There were no sufficient excuses for
    their actions, and in the end, both parents were charged with abuse."
11. The next transition follows at once: "Moving on to the next case where a
    mother was reckless enough to abandon her own child on the road, believing
    that twisting her story would save her from a harsh fate…"

That video was short and nearly all of it was used. The next one ran 35
minutes and made a 24-minute case, because the mother kept repeating the same
account and the officer the same questions: said once, the viewer understands
and is not worn out.

## Reference: how finished compilations open

Structure to follow; the wording belongs to those videos.

**When Killer Cops Realize They've Been Caught Red-Handed** (four cases)

- Three intro clips of 2-8 seconds.
- Hook: "Some disturbed cops believe they can escape justice after committing
  horrific crimes. But what happens when their plan falls apart, and the truth
  catches up to them?"
- Four intro clips of 1-4 seconds.
- Title line: "These were the moments when killer cops realized they’d been
  caught red-handed."
- First transition: "We begin with the case of a trouble-minded cop who
  believed that his fabricated story would be enough to protect him from the
  fallout of his own disturbing actions…" (two intro clips of 7-8 seconds).
- Case opening: "On October 18th, 2015, officers from the Palm Beach County
  Sheriff’s Office received an unusual call. The caller was one of their own,
  Officer Nouman Raja, who reported that he had just shot a man on the roadway.
  At the time, Nouman believed the incident had gone unnoticed, and as he
  called for help, he carefully positioned himself as the victim…" (a
  55-second clip).

**When GOOD Cops Go Pale After HORRIFYING Discovery** (four cases and a reuse)

- Four intro clips, the hook, four more intro clips, the title line.
- First transition: "We begin with a case where a simple traffic accident
  escalated into one of the most gruesome scenes officers had ever
  encountered…" (five intro clips of 1-2 seconds).
- Case opening: "On October 27, 2022, Roseville Police received a call about a
  traffic crash involving a tractor-trailer and a car. According to the caller,
  the car had run a red light and struck the semi-truck, but the driver fled
  the scene before authorities could be contacted. When officers arrived, both
  vehicles were still there, but what they didn’t yet know was that this wasn’t
  a simple accident – it was a case that would mark their careers…"
- Its last case is a reuse, so the script ends on a transition, with the link
  as a comment on it: "Up next, we have a case where a welfare check on a woman
  led to a gruesome discovery inside a freezer…".

**When GOOD Cops Discover Horrifying Crime Scenes** (three cases and two
reuses)

- After the third case's outro, two reuse cases close the script, each only a
  transition with its link as a comment: "That case was deeply unsettling, but the next one
  takes things even further, as a welfare check on a family escalated into a
  twisted investigation that revealed how far rage can drive a person…" and
  "Up next, we have a case where what began as a routine welfare check on a
  mother and her baby spiraled into a gruesome discovery at a lake…".
