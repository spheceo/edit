# Courtroom writing guide

A Courtroom video tells one case through court footage: a hearing or a
trial, sometimes with the bodycam or dashcam that was played in court. The
viewer watches the hearing unfold, from the backstory to the ruling.

The Cut-In (Canto) framework applies as it is: Anchor → Bridge → Teaser, the
outro, the restraint, outside sources, ellipses and the words to handle with
care. This guide changes what a Courtroom script needs differently: the
opening, how footage is chosen between cut-ins, the length of a cut-in, the
document, the heading and the voice. Where the two guides differ, follow this
one. The Cut-In guide's calm opening, its intro built on a clash, and its
"INTERRO" heading belong to Interrogation. Its three reference scripts are
interrogations: read them for how a cut-in hands over to footage, and take
the voice from the Courtroom references below.

read_script names the purpose of every cut-in. Write each to its purpose.

## The shape

A Courtroom script is mostly the hearing itself. Narration is short and
sparse: about 300 to 500 words in a video of about 20 to 25 minutes, in
three to five cut-ins after the opening.

1. **Intro clips.** Three to five short clips, about 25 to 45 seconds in
   all: the moments the title promises.
2. **The first cut-in: a tiny intro, then the backstory.** Two paragraphs
   with no footage between them.
3. **The hearing.** Long stretches of footage, each followed by a short
   cut-in, in source order.
4. **The outro.** After the final footage, the ruling and what it meant.

## Intro clips

The moments the title promises: the officer caught out on the stand, the
sharpest exchange, the judge's sharpest line. A few seconds each. One may run
longer, up to about 25 seconds, when it is the moment the whole video is
about: in Reference 4 the producer kept a 25-second clip because "it's the
most important clip where we actually see her in fear … and also mentions
the squatting story." Never show the ruling itself.

Every intro clip is a moment that also plays in the body, in its place in the
story. The one exception is a moment the producer brings in from another
video for the intro alone: in Reference 1 the producer framed the case "as
the cop getting caught lying" and asked for that moment, from a second
upload, in the intro.

## The intro

One or two sentences, about 20 to 45 words: the whole video's promise in a
line. Who, what they claimed, the moment it turned. Never the ruling. It can
open on the officer and the turn ("Swearing under oath that his bogus report
was completely flawless, a corrupt officer watched his entire case
implode…"), on the stakes in general ("Police officers are given immense
power to protect citizens, but some use that authority to enforce rules that
don't exist…"), or on the person the viewer is about to meet ("This is Jordan
Lepic, and she's currently in deep trouble all because of one person…").

## The case opening

The backstory, one full paragraph of about 80 to 150 words, past tense,
straight after the intro in the same cut-in, separated from it by a blank
line. The intro and the case opening are one cut-in: the narrator reads from
one into the other with no footage between.

Tell what the viewer needs before the hearing starts: when and where, who the
people are by full name and role, what happened before court (the stop, the
charge, the dispute), why it reached a courtroom, and what this hearing is.
Footage the video skips can be told here ("To open the trial, the defense
attorney cornered the officer on the stand by breaking down the Texas Penal
Code…"). End on the moment the first footage picks up, so the paragraph runs
straight into it.

## Choosing the footage

Between two cut-ins, let the hearing play a long section, usually four to
eight minutes, longer when an exchange holds. The hearing mostly plays
through, and its seams fall where the cut-ins go: in Reference 1 the body runs
from 0:47 to 25:15 with only seconds left out between blocks.

Court footage carries itself, so keep cut-ins few. The producer reviewed a
Courtroom cut with a cut-in every two or three minutes and said: "Seems like
the AI is set to make constant cut ins to keep the clip length down.
Courtroom is different. We can have longer clip sections, no need for so many
unnecessary text cut ins." The references below cut in more often than that;
take their voice, not their cadence. Place a cut-in only where the viewer
needs what the footage cannot give: a new person or witness to introduce, a
jump in time or place, another hearing or video, the ruling. Never place one
just to break up a long stretch, or to restate what the footage already
shows, and never interrupt a strong exchange to place one.

Do not stitch a block from many short pieces. Cut a stretch only to drop what
does not serve the story (a recess, a sidebar, paperwork, a long silence, an
exchange that repeats), and even then keep it to two or three pieces.

How tight the cuts are changes partway through the video. At first, cut
tight so someone is speaking almost all the time. Past halfway (in a
compilation, from the second case on), relax: group what you can into one timestamp range per block, keep the
natural pauses, and leave out only what does not serve the story and dead air
of five seconds or more. The producer found the second half of a cut read as
too many small timestamps, cut for words alone, when the hearing could simply
play.

Many court videos are other creators' uploads, with their own voice-over,
recaps, intro and requests to subscribe. Never keep any of it in a clip: in
Reference 4 the producer asked the editor to cut out "But that won't stop
him", said by the source's narrator. Never repeat a narrator's wording in the
narration either.

## The cut-ins

Short: two or three sentences, about 35 to 70 words. Anchor on what the last
stretch showed, bridge to where the hearing went next, and tease the next
block's turn, usually ending on an ellipsis ("…setting the stage for a
dramatic turning point…"). Name what the footage cannot: who someone is, what
a document says, what an exchange meant for the case. Never tell the same
fact twice.

Past tense is the default. Describing the footage the viewer is about to see
in the present tense also works, as Reference 1 does throughout its body
("Deputy Raymond takes the stand with full confidence…"); keep one tense
within a cut-in.

## The outro

State the ruling plainly with its specifics: the verdict, the fine or
judgment and how it was worked out, what the judge said to the officer. Say
who came out ahead. In Reference 4 the reviewer asked "So in conclusion who
actually won here?" of an outro that gave the numbers without saying it. It
may add what happened after (a letter, a dismissal, a reprimand), but no new
tease and nothing invented. One paragraph, or two when the ruling and its
aftermath both need room.

## Framing

The title sets the angle, and the script follows it, but never bends the
outcome. In Reference 1 the driver lost and paid a $120 fine; the video's
angle was the deputy's testimony, and the outro reports both: the ruling
against her, and the judge reprimanding him for testifying without reviewing
his own report and video.

## Getting the facts right

The reviewers read every script against the footage. These are the mistakes
they caught in "When Cocky Attorney TAUNTS The Judge.. BACKFIRES Massively!";
check each before you finish.

**Who is who.** Speaker labels and the names in a court transcript are the
transcription's guesses. At the bench several people speak in turn, so an
appearance ("Edward … on behalf of Corey Jones") can be misheard and land
under the wrong speaker. Before you name anyone, check it against what the
hearing itself says: a motion read aloud names who filed it, the judge
addresses people by name, the person answering is the one addressed. In the
Corey Jones case the transcript heard the second attorney, Edward Drukis,
introduce himself as "Edward Lucas", and the label gave that name to James
Murray, the attorney withdrawing, although the motion read in court named
Murray. The script swapped the two men throughout. When one name seems to
cover two people, or two names one person, sort it out before writing. If
you cannot, use the role ("his other attorney") and tell the producer. Never
explain a heard name away as a mishearing of someone else's name: "Drukus"
was not "Lucas"; it was Drukis.

The labels can also split one person into two. In the Judge Oakley case one
defense attorney argued from start to finish, but the transcript gave her two
speakers, and the script wrote "A second attorney from King's office then
stepped up…" It became "Denied twice, the same attorney pressed on…" Before
you write that someone new stepped in, or that the judge heard something from
a given person, check it: the judge addressing the same person, the same voice
answering, frames of who stands at the table. The same script said the judge
"had already heard a different story from the attorney King sent in his
place"; it was the prosecutor, who had spoken to King herself.

**Everyone named is introduced.** The first time the narration names a
person, say who they are to the case. If the footage that introduces them is
cut, the narration has to do it. An outro named Ian Conway with no word of
who he was; he is the CEO of Helios Global, the firm that held Young's
security clearance, and the footage where he first comes up had been left
out.

**Events in the order they happened.** When a sentence puts two events
together, keep them in the footage's order, and watch the words that set it:
"but not before", "after", "then", "first". "Judge Simpson granted the motion
and let him withdraw, but not before telling him not to come back to the
county" had it backwards: the judge granted the motion first, then warned
him.

**What was actually asked or ordered.** Name the request itself, and whom an
order or sanction is against. "Asked for both cases to follow the more
serious one" said nothing a viewer could follow; the attorney asked for an
adjournment so this case could follow the more serious one. A small word in
the transcript can change the meaning: the judge reserved sanctions "against
the lawyers of the parties", which the transcript heard as "the lawyers and
the parties", sanctions against Young and CNN themselves. When a fact turns
on one such word, or the transcript reads oddly for what is happening, take
the reading the rest of the hearing supports, or leave the claim out and tell
the producer.

**Not a fault:** a bridge that says who someone is and where the hearing
went, then ends on the tease, is not too much of a summary. The reviewer kept
"Before Jones could answer, the judge turned to Drukis…": the line about
Drukis gives context and the last sentence works as the teaser.

## The document

The Doc has the heading, the Assets and the total duration as always, then
the intro clips' line, the intro paragraph, and everything after it as one
running paragraph: the case opening, then each footage range inline followed
by its cut-in, to the outro. Write each cut-in so it reads on from the
footage before it in that paragraph.

## Heading

`COURT [verified full name] - [video title]`, naming the person the case is
about: the officer when the case turns on an officer's conduct or testimony,
otherwise the defendant or the party the case is brought against: "COURT
Jesse Raymond - COCKY Corrupt Cop Gets Caught LYING In Court — GOES HORRIBLY
WRONG!".

## Several videos

A case may come as several videos, joined in the order the producer gave: a
hearing and its follow-up (Reference 4's trial date), or another upload of
the same moment (Reference 1's intro). Tell the story in that order unless
the evidence shows otherwise, and use a moment that appears in two videos
once, from the better one. When the producer's link starts partway into a
long upload, the case they mean begins near there; footage long before it
may be another case on the same upload.

## A compilation: the title opens with "When"

A Courtroom title that opens with "When" ("When Cocky Attorney TAUNTS The
Judge.. BACKFIRES Massively!") is a compilation: every video is its own
court case, and each is there because it delivers the title. The Several
videos section above does not apply: never join two videos into one story,
and never carry a name, date or ruling from one case into another.

The compilation guide gives everything around the cases: the opening clips,
the hook, the title line and the transitions. This guide gives how each case
is told. What changes from a single Courtroom case:

- **No tiny intro.** The hook and the title line open the whole video, and
  each case starts with its transition, then its own short intro clips, then
  the case opening. The case opening is its own cut-in, the backstory alone,
  shorter than in a single case (about 60 to 120 words): who is in front of
  the judge and why, what happened before this hearing, ending on the moment
  the first footage picks up.
- **The hook is about courtrooms.** A general statement of the title's
  theme, then the question the video answers: "Some attorneys walk into
  court convinced the rules don't apply to them. But what happens when they
  push a judge one step too far?" No person, date or place.
- **Transitions point at the title.** "We begin with a case where a defense
  attorney tried to talk over a judge who had already caught him misleading
  the court…", "Up next, we have a case where an attorney's smirk at the
  bench cost him far more than the motion he came to argue…". Name what this
  attorney or party did, specifically; never give away the ruling.
- **Each case is a short hearing.** About 8 to 12 minutes and 1 to 3 footage
  blocks, played in long stretches as above. Cut-ins inside a case stay short
  (35 to 70 words).
- **Each case ends on its ruling.** The outro says what the judge decided,
  the sanction or consequence, and who came out ahead, with no tease. The
  next case's transition follows straight after it. No two outros open the
  same way: open each on its own case's consequence ("Murray got his
  withdrawal, along with a warning.", "No exam took place that day."). A
  script whose four outros all began "In the end," read as templated.
- **The heading is the title alone**, with COURT in front: the cases have
  different people, so none is named in it.

## Reference scripts

The producer's four finished Courtroom scripts. The intro clips are described
by their length, each footage block by its length; the narration is as
written, except that Reference 2's em dashes are commas here (narration never
uses one). Follow them for voice, how much each cut-in reveals, and how the
opening is built. Their people, dates, places and outcomes belong only to
those cases; never borrow them. References 3 and 4 still build some blocks
from several pieces; follow References 1 and 2 for how footage is chosen.

### Reference 1: a deputy's tailgating ticket

Intro clips: three clips, 26 seconds, from a second upload of the hearing:
the deputy caught out on the stand.

1. Swearing under oath that his bogus report was completely flawless, a
   corrupt officer watched his entire case implode when defense counsel
   played the damning footage catching him fabricating charges live in
   court.

   In July 2025, Deputy Jesse Raymond pulled over driver Jasmine Ray Adair on
   US 131, alleging she was tailgating another vehicle at sixty miles per
   hour. Believing the traffic ticket was completely unjustified, Jasmine
   fought the citation in court. When Deputy Raymond took the witness stand
   to defend his report, defense counsel launched a sharp cross-examination
   that completely unraveled the deputy's credibility.

Footage, 2:41.

2. Deputy Raymond takes the stand with full confidence, laying out his story
   of spotting a driver tailgating on a busy highway. But as he walks the
   court through how he caught up to the vehicle, defense counsel is already
   lining up a strategy to challenge his claims.

Footage, 3:17.

3. The deputy tries to back up his decision by leaning on his years of road
   experience, insisting the distance between the cars was unsafe. However,
   an immediate objection from the defense throws a wrench in his momentum,
   raising big questions about whether his claims hold any real weight.

Footage, 2:25.

4. When cross-examination hits, Deputy Raymond's confident story quickly
   begins to fall apart. Under sharp questioning, he's forced to admit he
   can't even remember basic details about the vehicles involved, setting the
   stage for a dramatic turning point…

Footage, 3:06.

5. Trapped on the stand, the deputy admits to quoting a specific driving rule
   to the driver, only for defense counsel to expose that his rule wasn't
   based on state law at all. As the officer gets backed into a corner over
   basic road conditions, the defense prepares to introduce critical
   evidence.

Footage, 3:41.

6. Defense counsel zeroes in on the road layout and the fact that the state
   failed to present key video evidence in court. Seeing an opening, the
   defense steps up to play their own footage right in front of the judge,
   putting the officer's credibility to the test.

Footage, 3:19.

7. The bodycam audio rings out across the courtroom, broadcasting the
   officer's own window-side statements directly to the bench. As the judge
   listens closely to what really happened on scene, the entire case hangs in
   the balance ahead of the final ruling.

Footage, 5:21, ending on the ruling.

8. The judge ruled that Jasmine Ray Adair was responsible for the civil
   infraction of following too closely, ordering her to pay a $120 fine.
   Before concluding, the judge severely reprimanded Deputy Raymond for
   failing to review his police report and video evidence before testifying,
   warning him never to sleepwalk through court again.

Narration about 440 words; one continuous clip per block.

### Reference 2: a driver charged with evading arrest

Intro clips: five clips, 38 seconds, from the cross-examination and the
closing argument.

1. A routine traffic stop turned into an intense courtroom battle after an
   overzealous cop accused a driver of evading arrest. But when the dashcam
   footage was played in front of the judge, the officer's own story quickly
   fell apart.

   This was the case of Jordan Davis, who found himself facing a serious
   charge of evading arrest after a routine drive took an unexpected turn.
   The prosecutor claimed Mr. Davis intentionally tried to run from law
   enforcement. However, the defense painted a completely different picture,
   arguing that Jordan was simply looking for a safe, well-lit place to pull
   over, just as official safety guidelines recommended. To open the trial,
   the defense attorney cornered the officer on the stand by breaking down
   the Texas Penal Code, pointing out that the state had to prove an actual
   intent to flee. That claim made no sense given that Jordan stayed within
   the speed limit, kept his headlights on, and turned on his hazard lights
   to signal the deputy. But as the attorney turned to the original reason
   for the pull-over, the officer's entire case began to crumble.

Footage, 2:08.

2. With the trial underway, the defense moved to challenge the deputy's
   initial excuse for pulling Jordan Davis over in the first place. As the
   attorney began dissecting the officer's claims on the stand, the state's
   foundation started to look shaky. But just as the officer tried to
   justify his actions using the dashcam footage, the defense set a
   brilliant trap regarding the actual road conditions that caught the
   deputy completely off guard.

Footage, 3:42.

3. Having already forced the deputy to admit that Jordan was driving past
   dark, unlit dirt roads with working headlights, the defense decided to
   push even harder. Instead of relying on opinions, the attorney pulled out
   an official state document to challenge the officer's version of proper
   driving behavior. What followed was a tense exchange that turned the
   prosecution's own safety rules right back against them.

Footage, 2:46.

4. Now that the official driving manual had put the officer on defense,
   attention shifted directly to Jordan's actual driving behavior during the
   encounter. The defense zeroed in on specific details from the dashcam,
   forcing the deputy to review the driver's exact movements step-by-step.
   As the clip played out, the officer was backed into a corner where he had
   to address Jordan's constant attempt to signal him.

Footage, 2:41.

5. Up to this point, the defense had systematically shown that Jordan kept
   his hazards on, maintained a steady speed, and avoided dangerous ditch
   stops. Now, the court finally got to see how the traffic stop actually
   wrapped up on video. But as the vehicle came to a complete halt, the
   officer's testimony about how Jordan behaved once stopped left the court
   with a massive decision to make.

Footage, 2:49.

6. Fast-forward to two days later, and court reconvened for closing
   arguments after the judge took time to review the full dashcam footage in
   its entirety. Building on every flaw exposed during cross-examination,
   from the missing dashcam proof to the proper use of hazard lights,
   Jordan's attorney delivered a fiery final statement. He laid out a
   compelling argument that framed Jordan's actions in a light the
   prosecution never saw coming.

Footage, 3:53.

7. With the defense wrapping up a masterclass in challenging a rogue traffic
   stop, the pressure shifted entirely to the state. Realizing their case
   was slipping away after all the admissions made on the stand, the
   prosecutor frantically scrambled to salvage the charges. The state
   launched into a series of technical arguments, desperately trying to
   convince the judge that slow compliance should still be treated as a
   crime.

Footage, 2:53.

8. The judge quickly cut through the prosecution's weak semantics, pointing
   out that Jordan Davis had no burden to prove he had read the handbook.
   After reviewing the dashcam footage in its entirety, she confirmed that
   Mr. Davis acted reasonably by seeking a safe, well-lit location, and
   officially delivered a verdict of not guilty, completely clearing him of
   all charges.

Narration about 660 words; one continuous clip per block. The case's
footage starts partway into a long upload.

### Reference 3: a cyclist arrested for leaving a blocked bike lane

Intro clips: four clips, 29 seconds, from the bodycam and the
cross-examination.

1. Police officers are given immense power to protect citizens, but some use
   that authority to enforce rules that don't exist…

   A late-night bike ride through Detroit turned into a bitter legal battle
   when Sergeant Brian Tershak pulled over cyclist David Pletz. Heavy
   construction had completely blocked the designated bike lane with large
   wooden signs, forcing Pletz to safely merge into the main street.
   However, the veteran officer ignored the hazardous road conditions,
   treating the situation purely as an act of defiance. What began as a
   routine stop quickly escalated into an immediate physical arrest,
   aggressive interrogation, and multiple traffic citations. Months later,
   both men stood in a courtroom where the judge reviewed the official video
   evidence frame-by-frame before putting the sergeant under intense
   cross-examination.

Footage (bodycam played in court), 2:16.

2. Pletz tried to explain that large construction signs were blocking his
   path. Instead of checking the road, Tershak demanded physical
   identification right away. What happened next was an immediate escalation
   that turned a simple safety discussion into pure chaos…

Footage, 3:44.

3. With Pletz forced against the patrol car and placed in handcuffs,
   additional units arrived on the scene. As the situation unfolded on
   camera, Tershak began talking to his fellow officer. What he said next
   revealed an attitude that would leave anyone listening completely
   speechless…

Footage, 2:38.

4. Tershak leaned into the car window to address the detainee one last time.
   What he handed over through that window set the stage for a massive
   courtroom showdown…

Footage, 2:09.

5. Transitioning from the body camera footage back to the live trial, the
   defense attorney called Sergeant Tershak to the witness stand. Under
   oath, the officer stood by his original police report. But as the defense
   lawyer started asking questions, he set a subtle trap that was about to
   catch the officer completely off guard…

Footage, 3:21.

6. Continuing on the witness stand, Tershak watched as the attorney replayed
   the exact moment of the stop on court monitors. The defense pressed him
   hard on whether a cyclist has the legal right to avoid hazardous
   construction. The officer's attempt to defend his actions under oath
   created an uncomfortable moment in the courtroom…

Footage, 3:15.

7. When the defense attorney asked Sergeant Tershak if an officer can ignore
   written traffic laws, the officer refused to back down. As he tried to
   explain his actions on the stand, his answer led to a sudden
   confrontation nobody expected…

Footage, 3:57, ending on the ruling.

8. After reviewing all the body camera and scout car video evidence
   presented during the trial, Judge Malaika Ramsey-Heath delivered her
   final ruling. She noted that state law explicitly allows cyclists to exit
   designated bike lanes to safely navigate around road hazards and
   obstacles. Because construction signs clearly blocked the path, Sergeant
   Tershak's order to remain in the bike lane was not a lawful directive.
   Finding that David Pletz acted fully within his legal rights, the judge
   declared him not guilty on all counts.

Narration 463 words; runtime about 21.5 minutes.

### Reference 4: a TikTok influencer evicted for $20,000 in unpaid rent (two videos)

landlord in fear, explaining the squatting story).

1. This is Jordan Lepic, and she's currently in deep trouble all because of
   one person, Taversia Borrell. Taversia is best known as a TikTok
   influencer and content creator, but today you'll see a different side of
   her. To understand the current case, we need a bit of backstory.

   The Lepics never wanted to be landlords. They wanted to sell. And they
   had a buyer, Taversia and her partner, Andrew Elliott, under contract on
   the Lepics' house, two days from closing. But then the sale fell apart.
   Taversia and Andrew asked to move in anyway. The Lepics said yes, if
   Taversia paid the mortgage. They drew up a short lease off the purchase
   agreement, expecting the sale to come back together any week, but it
   didn't. Sixty days became an extension. The extension became another
   short-term lease. And somewhere in that stack of paperwork, the rent
   simply stopped coming, for 4 months. This added up to roughly $20,000 in
   unpaid rent. The buyers had become tenants, and then stopped paying,
   effectively becoming a full blown squatter. By the time it reached a
   courtroom, the Lepics were the ones in trouble, because Taversia knew
   exactly what she was doing…
