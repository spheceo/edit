# Cut-In writing framework

The producer calls this the Canto/cut-in framework. Narration provides context
and direction; footage supplies evidence and emotion. If footage or dialogue
already conveys a fact, do not restate it. Add meaning that the viewer would
otherwise miss, then create a reason to keep watching.

## The two-split intro

A video opens in two parts, with the first cut-in between them:

1. **Calm opening.** One or two clips, rarely three, about 9–12 seconds in all.
   The case quietly begins: an interrogator sitting down and giving their name,
   an officer arriving. Nothing dramatic is said yet.
2. **Intro cut-in** (cut-in 1).
3. **Hook.** 15–30 seconds of the most striking things said anywhere in the
   footage.
4. **Case-opening cut-in** (cut-in 2), then the story in order.

## Strict cut-in sequence

Determine the cut-in number and purpose from read_script before writing.

1. **Intro.** Two or three short sentences, about 40 to 55 words, heard in the
   first seconds of the video. Its job is to shock, then to open a second
   question, and to get there fast. Refer to the person in plain terms ("This
   man", "This mother") or by name, and build the paragraph as two turns:
   - **The clash.** Set an expectation beside the fact that breaks it. Take the
     expectation from something specific and sourced about this person or
     case, never from a generality. "She has no criminal record and looks
     completely harmless. Yet just minutes ago, she fired a loaded handgun
     directly at a man's back as he walked away." "This man had just brutally
     killed his wife, yet he thought he could slip under the officers' radar by
     deceiving them." If the person is a college freshman, find what in this
     case makes that matter; do not write that students rarely kill.
   - **The second turn.** Then show there is more to it than the shock, or
     name the thing that will undo them. "But what officers don't realise is
     that this isn't a sudden, random act of violence." "But one small slip in
     his story was all it took for his carefully crafted act to start
     unraveling." The viewer should be left asking why, or how it falls apart.
     A line that only says the story gets worse is not enough.
   - Leave out what any viewer would assume: that a suspect is being
     questioned, that he walks the detective through the night step by step,
     that officers are investigating. Every clause either shocks or raises the
     question.
   - Get to the point. One detail carries each turn; a second one drags. Cut
     amounts, place names, officers' names and step-by-step lead-up unless the
     shock depends on them: those belong to the case opening and later
     cut-ins. "Just hours ago, this college freshman offered a classmate gas
     money for a ride. That classmate is now dead in a ditch." needs no price,
     no store and no town. If the draft runs past three sentences, cut it.
   - This is the one cut-in that leans on the present tense ("Deputies are
     taking her into custody. She has no criminal record…"). Do not force it,
     but write as if it is happening now.
   - Never describe a person's physical appearance or what they wear ("in a
     trench coat"). No reference does.
   - Never describe their manner ("calm, polite, answers every question with
     'sir'"). The footage shows it.
   - Place the scene where the opening footage actually is. Read the transcript
     around the opening clips: someone being questioned in an interview room has
     not "just been arrested on the side of a highway", whatever an article says
     about where the arrest took place.
   - Promise only what the sourced story delivers. Never invent atmosphere,
     time of day, weather or poetic framing: "before sunrise" is not a source.
2. **Case opening.** Start the account from the beginning: "On [verified
   date], [agency] …", the place, and how the case reached police. Introduce a
   person with full name and age when they first matter, not as a list of
   everyone involved. Hand over to what the next footage shows.
3. **Story development.** The remaining middle cut-ins use Anchor → Bridge →
   Teaser. Reveal new context progressively, building on the established story.
4. **Outro** (the conclusion role). Only the final cut-in closes the story,
   and it should feel final: what happened after the footage ends and the
   furthest verified outcome ("In the end, …": the arrest, the charges, the
   plea, the sentence). Tell it as the end of the story, not as a reading of
   the records: never narrate the paperwork ("the police file lists the case
   as cleared", "the released records do not show…") or what is still open in
   it (forensic results, a pending investigation). When nothing verified comes
   after the arrest or charge, end on that step, stated plainly, without
   saying that the rest is unknown. Never invent a verdict, plea or sentence,
   no new tease and no unsourced claim of acquittal. End on the decisive step
   and stop there: leave out what came after it when it leaves the case
   hanging or reopens it (how a resignation was timed, that no charges were
   ever filed, a later job, where the person is now). "…he failed the test,
   admitted to the allegations and resigned, leaving the department on
   November 22, 2013." ends a case; adding "No charges were ever filed, and he
   went on to land a top job…" undoes it. Study how each reference ends.

**Tense.** Everything after the intro is written in the past tense throughout.
The intro is the only exception.

**Length.** One paragraph per cut-in: a small chunk of text. Some reference
cut-ins run to two or three paragraphs; do not copy that. Never ramble. Say
what the viewer needs, then hand over to the footage.

For a single-marker draft, write the intro; add the other roles as the assembly
expands. With only two markers, the final outro takes precedence; do not fold a
case opening into the intro to compensate.

## Tension in the first minutes

The first three to five minutes of the finished video decide whether the
viewer stays, so the narration there holds information back. The structure
does not change: the intro, the hook, the case opening on its date, and in a
compilation the hook, the title line and the transitions all stay where and
as they are. What changes is how much each paragraph inside them gives away.

- **Gatekeep the payoff.** Say that something exists, never what it is: the
  evidence detectives are holding, the contradiction about to surface, the
  answer the next clip delivers. "But the detective isn't asking about that
  camera by chance…", not "But a license plate camera has already logged that
  truck at 3:22 in the morning…" when the confrontation comes twenty minutes
  later.
- **Let the footage land the reveal.** If a detective or the suspect is about
  to say it on camera, the narration before it does not.
- **Know more than the suspect, show less than you know.** That detectives
  are holding something he does not know about is tension; what it is can
  wait.
- **Open more than you close.** Each paragraph in these minutes should leave
  the viewer with a question the next stretch of footage keeps alive, not one
  it answers at once.
- **Cut what does not pull.** In these minutes a sentence either raises the
  stakes or sets up a question. Background the viewer does not yet need moves
  to a later cut-in or goes. A short case opening that ends on a question beats
  a long one that lays out the whole night.
- **In a compilation**, a transition still names the theme and what this
  person did, specifically, but stops before how they were caught. The case's
  intro clips and its first cut-ins carry that.

What is held back is released later, when the footage reaches it or in a later
cut-in's anchor; never tease something the script does not pay off. Tension
comes from timing, not from adjectives or invented suspense: sourced facts only,
plain words, no crime-novel drama. After the first minutes the cut-ins follow
Anchor → Bridge → Teaser as usual. This holds for interrogation scripts, single
cases and compilations alike; a FOIA script follows its own guide.

### How Red File holds information back

The producer pointed to the Red File channel as the model for these minutes.
Their intros share a few moves. They are patterns to borrow, not lines to copy:
Red File narrates far more than a cut-in does, and invents atmosphere ("The
roads are quiet") that this guide does not allow.

- **What the title promises is confirmed; what it does not say is kept.** The
  title already tells the viewer who did it, so the intro does not hide that.
  It hides how they are caught, what the police have, and when it surfaces.
- **The irony sits in one line.** They show the person's own denial or story,
  then state the gap between it and what is coming, without its content: "At
  the time she gave that interview, investigators had already been building
  the case around her for months. She just didn't know that yet."
- **Name the category, not the item.** "Every phone record, every financial
  trail, every text message she had ever written" tells the viewer the
  evidence exists; which text, and what it says, waits for the footage.
- **Point at a later moment without its answer.** "But later in the same
  interview, detectives begin asking a very different question." The viewer
  now waits for that question.
- **Ask the question outright, then leave it open.** "So if she wasn't
  involved, how did she know what happened to the body?" No answer follows in
  the same paragraph.
- **One step at a time.** Each paragraph adds one new fact and opens one new
  question: "What investigators did not know yet was that the reason had been
  documented in writing for several years." The writing is revealed minutes
  later, by the witness on camera.
- **Weigh a detail without concluding from it.** "It was a small
  inconsistency, the kind that proves nothing on its own. But detectives noted
  it."

### Before and after, from the producer's scripts

A case opening that gave away the payoff clip fifteen minutes early:

- Before: "…Undersheriff J.T. Palmer arrested Murray about three miles away
  on Highway 177. Before Palmer had asked him a single question, Murray told
  him he was the one they were looking for."
- After: "…A short time later, Undersheriff J.T. Palmer arrested Murray about
  three miles away on Highway 177. What Murray told him on the side of that
  road was the reason they were now sitting in an interview room…"

A middle cut-in that explained the trap before the detective set it:

- Before: "What he didn't know was that a Flock license plate camera… had
  recorded his truck heading north at 3:22 that morning. Now the detective gave
  him one chance to explain it…"
- After: "But the detective wasn't asking about cameras by chance. Before the
  interview was over, Mario would get one chance to explain what one of them
  had seen…" The camera, the place and the time come in a later cut-in, once
  the footage has played his denial.

### Reference 1: a husband questioned about his missing wife

1. This man had just brutally killed his wife, yet he thought he could slip
   under the officers’ radar by deceiving them. But one small slip in his story
   was all it took for his carefully crafted act to start unraveling.
2. On February 11, 2020, officers from the Shelby Police Department received a
   911 call from Lisa Davis, reporting that her friend, 22-year-old Gabe
   Clevenger, was missing. Gabe was supposed to show up at her workplace early
   that day, but failed to do so, raising suspicions among her friends.
3. According to Lisa, Gabe’s husband, 21-year-old Alec Blair, was the last
   person to see her. Lisa described Alec as unstable and said she was worried
   about Gabe. Officers began investigating Alec to understand what had happened
   between them, but his reaction to Gabe’s disappearance immediately raised red
   flags for the investigators.
4. Alec appeared completely fine, as if nothing had happened to his wife. He
   laughed, chatted about his personal life, and didn’t show a single sign of
   concern for Gabe’s well-being — a cold, detached, and disturbingly ruthless
   demeanor.
5. Alec’s statements about their relationship weren’t entirely false — just
   painfully incomplete. Yes, he punched walls, but it didn’t stop there. He
   would corner Gabe in rooms, smash her belongings, and create an environment
   of constant fear. Gabe wasn’t just tired of him; she was terrified.
6. Gabe had decided to move out of the trailer to run away from Alec – and
   that’s when she found love elsewhere. According to Alec, he knew nothing
   about the other man, but the reality was very different. Alec had stalked
   him, threatened him, and even sent him new pictures of Gabe. Investigators
   were aware of this and didn’t fall for his act of the innocent husband.
7. Alec told officers that someone had broken into their trailer and destroyed
   their belongings, but Gabe’s friends had a different story to tell. According
   to them, Alec himself had entered the trailer and started smashing her
   belongings, causing Gabe to move out. At the time, there wasn’t enough
   evidence against Alec, and since his name was still on the lease, he remained
   free. Now, investigators wanted to press him further and make him reveal
   exactly what had happened that day.
8. According to Alec, Gabe suddenly asked him to let her out of the car, even
   though they were in the middle of finalizing their divorce. Earlier, he had
   insisted that he wanted everything settled as quickly as possible – so why
   would he leave her alone on the side of the road over a small argument? The
   pieces didn’t fit together, and officers could feel the cracks in his story.
9. Alec’s version was full of lies. He knew CJ, Gabe’s boyfriend, well because
   he had been obsessively stalking him. He also knew Lisa, who reported Gabe’s
   disappearance and was CJ’s mother. After receiving threats from Alec and
   watching him break her belongings inside the trailer, Gabe moved out to stay
   with Lisa. That only fueled Alec’s anger, especially once he realized his
   “wife” was now involved with another man.
10. Alec claimed he had panic attacks after realizing Gabe was missing, but his
    cold demeanor during the interrogation didn’t support that at all. The
    entire time, he remained calm and relaxed – nothing like someone whose wife
    had just disappeared. Investigators noticed this immediately, and now they
    were determined to uncover the full truth.
11. At this point, just as the interrogation was heating up, the cameras
    suddenly stopped working, and none of Alec’s earlier confessions had been
    recorded. Once the investigators realized this, they needed him to admit
    everything again. But their approach had to change. Now, the investigator
    leaned back, hands resting on his head, trying to appear calm and relaxed in
    the hope that Alec would repeat his confession once more.
12. His lies were quickly falling apart, and officers soon uncovered a major
    contradiction. The blood in the car wasn’t from his friend’s nose as Alec
    claimed, it was Gabe’s. He had insisted that he only hit his wife, but new
    findings would soon reveal that even this was a lie, because something far
    worse would come to light.
13. Alec had just killed his wife and was confessing it for the second time,
    even smiling at moments. Now, investigators needed him to reveal exactly
    where Gabe was.
14. Alec’s behavior was deeply disturbing. He pointed to the spot where Gabe’s
    body lay as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world – a chilling
    sign of how sick his mind had become. This wasn’t an accident. Alec knew
    exactly what he was doing, and he showed no remorse.
15. Despite Alec’s attempts to change the story and fool the investigators, they
    weren’t buying it. His order of the facts made no sense to them. If what
    happened was intentional, why did he leave Gabe’s body lying in the field
    without any possibility of help?
16. After the interrogation, Alec led the investigators to where Gabe was.
    There, in the middle of the cornfield, they found her lifeless body.
    However, the condition of her body was very different from Alec’s account.
    Gabe had been stabbed 14 times, proving that she did not pass away from a
    single punch, as Alec had claimed during the interrogation. In the end, Alec
    pleaded guilty to 14 charges, including aggravated murder, and was sentenced
    to 45 years to life in prison.

## Anchor → Bridge → Teaser

**Anchor:** briefly orient a viewer who looked away during the previous block.
Choose the style that fits the beat, and vary it across the script:

- Time/state jump: a phase has ended or the situation has changed.
- Behavior: the character of a subject's response, grounded in the source.
- Officer POV: what officers are doing, noticing or explicitly realizing.
- Contextual reveal: a verified person or fact that reframes the moment.
- Ongoing action: a compact, meaningful clause that keeps us in the scene.

An anchor is not a literal recap of the conversation. Do not infer hidden motives,
thoughts or guilt from demeanor. Officer realizations and interpretations must
be supported by dialogue, inspected footage or saved research evidence.

**Bridge:** advance the beat with a contradiction, observation, realization,
decision or relevant outside context. Either the unfolding investigation or
verified reporting can supply this. Read the surrounding transcript and saved
assets first. Integrate background naturally; do not pause for a biography,
stack prior arrests or turn three sentences into an information dump. Spread
necessary context across appropriate beats. Keep allegations, charges and
established outcomes distinct. A proposed title is not evidence.

**Teaser:** end on a specific shift that the next footage block actually pays
off. Create curiosity without announcing the answer. Neither generic promises
("what happened next") nor resolving the current beat creates useful pull.
Read the next block before teasing it. The final paragraph is the exception:
close on the furthest verified outcome as the story's end, without narrating
the records or what is still pending; do not promise footage that does not
exist.

## Outside sources

When a cut-in draws on something outside the footage, such as court papers, a
police report or later reporting, name the source first and let what it says
arrive as a discovery: "According to court papers, it would become clear
that…". Do not write "Court papers would later say…". The same shape holds
for any outside source.

## Ellipses

Use an ellipsis ("…") to leave a beat of space: at the end of most cut-ins that
hand over to footage, and on some sentences inside them where a thought is left
hanging. Not everywhere. A plain statement of fact ends on a full stop, and the
final outro does too.

## Words to handle with care

Some words are muted or blurred in the finished video, so write them only when
the story needs them:

sex, sexual, abuse, neglect, nude, nudity, pornography, kill, murder, stabbed,
suicide, rape, raped, rapist, molest, molested, molestation, lascivious, lewd,
sexual assault, sexual battery, child porn, child pornography, pedophile, child
predator, incest.

- Never pair one of them with "child": write "neglect" or "abuse", not "child
  neglect" or "child abuse". It matters most when children are involved.
- Starvation words (starve, starving, starved, malnourished) count the same way
  when they are about a child.
- You do not mark them yourself. Repack finds them in the saved narration and
  adds the note to the editor to blur each one.
- Prefer the plainer word when it says the same thing.

Harm done to a child is told by what it meant, never by the blow. The
producer: "Describing child abuse like this is something that can cause issues
for us", "Be careful about describing the boy being hurt too much in detail…
such as 'hit him in the head.'" It holds in every cut-in, and a change asked
for in one paragraph applies wherever the same kind of wording appears.

- Not this: "drag a little boy from her car and start hitting him". Instead:
  "turn on a little boy who wouldn't get out of her car for the bus".
- Not this: "She admitted hitting him on the head" or "it went well beyond a
  spanking". Instead: "She admitted disciplining him over a tantrum", "it went
  far beyond ordinary discipline".
- Not this: "exactly how one punch had sent her into the corner of a dresser",
  "her distress wasn't only about the punch". Instead: "how she had been hurt
  and what had led up to it", "went deeper than her injury".
- Not this: "scars consistent with ligature marks, thick scar tissue".
  Instead: "signs of long-term harm".

Never write "predator", "predatory" or "predators" anywhere in the narration,
even when the title uses the word. Say it around it, in descriptive words that
leave the viewer in no doubt: "deeply disturbing officers", "an officer with a
dark secret", "a deputy who targeted young girls" (when the case supports it).

- Not this: "Police officers are trusted to protect the public, yet some hide
  dark, predatory secrets behind the badge."
- Not this: "These were the moments when predator cops were exposed by their
  own partners…"
- Instead: "These were the moments when deeply disturbing officers were
  exposed by their own partners…"

## Restraint

Avoid describing an obvious visible action, repeating a confession or other
on-camera dialogue, announcing the camera ("as seen in this clip"), narrating
routine police procedure, and irrelevant weather, uniforms, vehicles or
addresses. A detail earns a place only when it changes the story's meaning.
Keep language concrete and restrained; no invented inner monologues,
sensational speculation or unsupported certainty.

## Before saving each paragraph

1. Does the anchor orient the viewer to this beat?
2. Does the bridge add a relevant insight or sourced context?
3. Does the teaser point toward a specific coming shift without spoiling it?
4. Can footage communicate any sentence better? If so, remove it.
5. Would omitting this paragraph cost the viewer useful understanding?

Use these questions to revise, not as headings or labels in the final prose.
Read the first/last words of neighboring blocks so the narration fits the cut.
Write natural paragraphs; never put timing ranges or stage directions inside
the narration itself. Propose through write_cut_ins, tied to existing cut-in IDs.
The producer reviews a text diff before accepting the changes. Do not describe
proposed narration as applied. read_script includes pending draft wording for review.
Written narration is a script draft, not recorded voice-over. The marker remains
the existing three-second cut-in card until production records the narration.

## Revising an existing story

Read the ENTIRE saved script before adding paragraphs: note the established facts,
where information is first revealed, each teaser's payoff, and the conclusion.
Revise narration when the producer requests writing changes. Routine footage
edits preserve the saved paragraphs. Read the newly selected footage and the
beats on both sides; do not rely only on the short transcript overview.

Preserve the introduction unless the producer requests a rewrite or a sourced
factual correction is necessary. Fill the missing cut-ins, then revise only the
neighboring bridges/teasers that no longer fit. Do not introduce the same fact
twice or repeat an explanation viewers already heard. Later narration may recall
a fact briefly only when it adds a new implication.

Keep exactly one conclusion on the final cut-in. When new footage follows the
old conclusion, transfer its supported outcome to the final marker and adapt it
to the full story. Clear or rewrite its previous marker as a bridge. If the last
footage has no marker, insert_cut_in after the final source clip first. Save the
transfer and related paragraph revisions together with write_cut_ins, assigning
intro, bridge or conclusion roles. The case opening uses the bridge storage role,
but its structural purpose is explicitly supplied by read_script. Empty text clears an obsolete paragraph.

Preserve source ranges while writing. Prefer targeted edits when adjusting a
written story; if a rebuild is requested, reuse the existing written cut-in IDs.
New paragraph text must fit the new order; re-read the saved script at the end to
check chronology, repeated facts, premature outcomes and unfulfilled teasers.

## Document format from the reference scripts

- One large bold title: `INTERRO [verified subject, if known] - [video title]`.
  Leave out the subject slot when it is not established; never borrow the people
  or facts from an example script.
- An `Assets:` list with Footage, then a single `Article` hyperlink to the most informative reliable report, and
  directly linked `Suspect Image`, `Victim Image`, `Scene Image` or `Evidence Image`
  when available. At most one per role. Use read_assets then curate_assets.
  Every image needs publisher evidence tying it to this exact case; generic
  lookalikes, stock photography and identity guesses from faces are excluded.
  Image links point to imageUrl, not the publisher article. Research candidates
  stay available for evidence even when not selected for the document.
- Each footage block is a line of linked `(m:ss-m:ss)` ranges joined by ` + `,
  in timeline order, followed by the narration paragraph for its cut-in.
- Calm opening first, the intro cut-in, the hook, the case-opening cut-in, then
  the case's chronological beats and a final sourced outcome. Retain the producer's current selections
  unless they asked for an edit.
