# FOIA writing guide

A FOIA video tells one case through police footage released on request:
bodycam, dashcam, the station. There is no interview room. The viewer rides
along from the officers' arrival to the outcome.

The Cut-In (Canto) framework applies as it is: Anchor → Bridge → Teaser, the
outro, the tense, the restraint, outside sources, ellipses and the words to
handle with care. This guide changes what a FOIA script needs differently:
the opening, the video's length, the length of a cut-in, the heading, and
the voice. Where the two guides differ, follow this one. The Cut-In guide's
calm opening, its intro built on a clash, and its "INTERRO" heading belong to
Interrogation. Its three reference scripts are interrogations: read them for
how a cut-in hands over to footage, and take the voice from the FOIA
references below.

read_script names the purpose of every cut-in. Write each to its purpose.

## Two ways to open

The size of the case decides the opening. The producer may name one; follow
them when they do.

**One run of intro clips, for a small case.** One suspect, one scene, one
thing found: a man whose laptop held images, a single arrest. Four or five of
the most striking moments, about 20 to 30 seconds in all, then the case
opening. The first cut-in is the case opening; there is no separate intro.

**The two-split intro, for a bigger case.** A death, harm to a child, several
people, several scenes. The first clips are the person the title is about,
then the initial contact (officers arriving, the first words with the person
or the caller) and at most one striking line. Then the intro cut-in. Then
the rest of the striking moments, then the case opening.

The references show both. Merrill and Johnson open on one run of clips;
Garcia and Salazar take the two-split intro. Treat the split as a guide to
what the case needs, not a rule by subject: a child case with one scene and a
short, clear story may still open on one run.

Either way:

- Intro clips are quick proof, a few seconds each. A clip that runs long
  stalls them; find a shorter line that makes the same point.
- Every intro clip is a moment that also plays in the body, in its place in
  the story. The intro is a promise the footage keeps.
- Show the people at the centre of the case and the scene itself. Officers
  only talking about what they found is not enough: in the Garcia script the
  producer replaced such a clip with "something from the inside of the house
  … or confrontation with the parents. We need to see the parents." The person
  the title is about appears in the very first clips, before the officers: it
  was the producer's most common change to a finished FOIA intro. In the bank
  scam case the producer asked "to have clips of the girl in the very
  beginning of the intro", and in the RV twins case for the line about the
  pictures of the babies first, then footage from the RV itself.
- Open on the strongest line, the one that makes the viewer stay.

## The intro (two-split only)

One or two sentences, about 30 to 45 words. Present or present perfect: the
case is happening now. Set the situation as it first looked, then turn to what
it became, and end on an ellipsis. No date, no names, and no more place than
the city: those belong to the case opening that follows.

> Albuquerque police officers have just responded to what initially appeared
> to be a routine welfare check. But within minutes of arrival, they'd uncover
> conditions so disturbing that the investigation quickly turned into
> something far more sinister…

## The case opening

Past tense, about 50 to 65 words. Open on the verified date, then what
happened and where: "On December 1, 2024, a passerby in Panama City, Florida,
heard two small boys calling out from a broken bedroom window…". The date is
required; the producer flags a case opening without one. Say how the case
reached police, introduce a person by full name and age when they first
matter, and end on a tease of what the next footage shows, with an ellipsis.

When the case opens on one run of clips, the case opening is the first thing
the viewer hears, so its tease must pull as hard as an intro would: "But when
he opened the photo folder to back it up, he realized the customer had
forgotten exactly what he was handing over…"

The producer's own shape for it: who responded, where and why, then one
sentence that hints at what the title promises, then the tease. Tell it in
plain words, in the order it happened, as you would tell someone the story.

- Not this: "On August 22, 2024, Bernalillo County Sheriff's Detective Kevin
  Carhart arrived at a home in Albuquerque's South Valley for a welfare check
  on 87-year-old Isabel Martinez. He had just been assigned the case after a
  string of calls about the household. But the relatives who lived with her
  had already had deputies at their door twice that week, and they wanted to
  know why…" It opens on an officer and his paperwork, and says nothing of
  the neglect the title is about. The producer: "detectives from xx county
  responded to xx - and then we need to briefly mention something that hints
  that her relatives are severely neglecting her".
- Instead: "On August 22, 2024, deputies from the Bernalillo County Sheriff's
  Office responded to a home in Albuquerque's South Valley for a welfare check
  on 87-year-old Isabel Martinez. She shared the house with her niece and
  great-nephew, yet according to the sheriff's office report, the condition of
  that home and the injuries on Isabel suggested no one was truly caring for
  her. But the niece who answered the door had already had enough of police at
  her door…"
- Not this: "…Now staff were suspicious, so an assistant manager stalled her
  at the counter. She had no idea who was on the way…" The producer: "That's
  not our type of writing."
- Instead: "…Soon after, she walked into the TD Bank in Pompano Beach to try
  her luck again, this time for $6,400. But an assistant manager quickly
  caught on to what she was trying to do, and called the police…"

## The story

Each cut-in is one paragraph of about 50 to 65 words, past tense, Anchor →
Bridge → Teaser, ending on an ellipsis. Anchor on what the last block showed
and what it meant, bridge to what officers learned or decided, tease the next
block's shift. The first block after the case opening runs about one to two
minutes, so the first cut-in of the story comes quickly; after it, blocks run
about three minutes.

Name what the footage cannot: the person's full name and age, what a later
report found, what the officers' words meant in practice ("The door wasn't
held by an ordinary latch but by a steel cable, and according to later
reports, the boys had been shut in like this for over a year…").

Never tell the same fact twice. The producer flagged a fact mentioned in one
cut-in and again in the next; say it once, where it first matters.

Officers are "deputies", "officers" or "the detective", never named, as in
the references. When a script named "Detective Kevin Carhart" and later
"Detectives Kristopher Tuell and Derek Gallegos", the producer asked "who is
carhart?? we dont know that", then: "write deputies rather than writing the
name of the officer". Name the people the case is about, and a caller or
witness who matters ("Joe Todd's account…"), where the footage first shows
them.

## The outro

Short and final. Usually one or two sentences: "In the end, Gary G. Johnson
was arrested and charged with possession of …". It may add what happened to
the others in the case (who took the children, who else was charged), as
Merrill's does, but no new tease and nothing pending. The final footage block
comes before it, as always.

## Length

Aim for 20 to 30 minutes. Never go under 10. When the footage the story can
use runs short, the video keeps longer pauses rather than drop below 10
minutes: the usual tightening of silence gives way to the floor. Reach the
length with the story's own footage first, never with repetition or invented
narration. The references below ran 17 to 21 minutes, before this aim: follow
them for voice and shape, not length.

## Heading

`FOIA [verified full names, joined with " & "] - [video title]`, the names of
the people charged when they are known: "FOIA Kathy M. Merrill & John W.
Merrill - Cops Surprise Parents After They Lock Their Sons, 4 & 5, In
Roach-Infested Bedroom Cell".

## Reference scripts

The producer's three finished FOIA scripts, in order. Each footage block is
described by its length; the narration is as written. Follow them for voice,
how much each cut-in reveals, and how the opening is built. Their people,
dates, places and outcomes belong only to those cases; never borrow them.

### Reference 1: parents who locked their sons in a bedroom (one run of clips)

Intro clips: five clips, 24 seconds: the caller, the room, the deputies'
reactions ("room … unacceptable").

1. On December 1, 2024, a passerby in Panama City, Florida, heard two small
   boys calling out from a broken bedroom window, begging for help. They said
   they were locked in their room and needed to use the bathroom. When
   deputies arrived, what they learned about that locked door only got more
   disturbing by the minute…
2. The woman seen here was identified as 27-year-old Kathy M. Merrill, the
   mother of the two boys, ages four and five. To Kathy, the locked door was
   nothing more than a nighttime routine, and she spoke about it as if it were
   the most normal thing in the world. But as deputies made their way toward
   that bedroom, her calm explanation was about to fall apart…
3. Whatever the Merrills called it, deputies now understood what that lock
   meant in practice. The door wasn't held by an ordinary latch but by a steel
   cable, and according to later reports, the boys had been shut in like this
   for over a year… The man who answered their cries had a troubling detail of
   his own: it wasn't the first time he'd seen them at that window…
4. Joe Todd's account turned one desperate morning into a pattern. If the boys
   had called out from that window before, the locked door was no one-time
   lapse. It was how they lived. Yet when John spoke with deputies, he insisted
   the real danger to his sons wasn't behind their bedroom door at all…
5. The more John explained, the more convinced he seemed that he'd done the
   responsible thing. To him, a broken front door and a busy road made the
   lock a necessity. To deputies, it explained nothing about the room the boys
   were kept in. And John was about to find out which of those views would
   decide his morning…
6. John still couldn't accept what was happening. In his mind, he was the
   father keeping his son off the road, not the one who'd left both boys
   without a bathroom all night. But while he argued his case, a far bigger
   decision was being made about his sons, one neither parent would get a say
   in…
7. Officers could not tell Kathy and John whether the brothers would stay
   together. The parents hoped relatives might take them in, even as they
   argued that money problems and repairs had left them with few options. But
   the boys' immediate placement was no longer theirs to decide. John still
   had another question: what would happen to him at the sheriff's office…
8. The boys were placed in protective care. Kathy and John Merrill were each
   arrested and charged with two counts of neglect. Their grandfather, Michael
   Beaubien, lived right next to the boys' room and told deputies he knew
   about both the lock and the conditions inside. He was charged with failing
   to report neglect.

Runtime about 21 minutes; blocks between cut-ins ran 2 to 4 minutes.

### Reference 2: a man whose laptop held images of children (one run of clips)

Intro clips: five clips, 22 seconds: "do … saved", "over … 5000", "why …
drunk".

1. On July 31, 2025, a man in Stevens Point, Wisconsin, dropped his laptop off
   for repair with one simple request: make sure his pictures got saved. It
   was a job the technician had done countless times. But when he opened the
   photo folder to back it up, he realized the customer had forgotten exactly
   what he was handing over…
2. The technician had done nothing more than what the customer asked. But what
   he saw in those thumbnails was enough to make him call the police, and to
   agree to help them reach the laptop's owner. Before officers could act on
   it, though, they needed to know exactly how much of that folder he had
   actually seen…
3. The technician had seen only a small part of the folder, and he could not
   say what every file contained. Police had the laptop, but its owner had yet
   to explain the collection. The next day, officers sat down with Gary G.
   Johnson, the man who had asked for those pictures to be saved…
4. Gary seemed to have an explanation ready. In his version, the folder was
   material for a publishing project, built entirely from other people's
   photos, and not one of them was taken by him. To officers, that raised more
   questions than it answered. When they asked what exactly he had been
   searching for, one detail kept coming up…
5. No matter how the question was framed, Gary steered back to photography.
   Officers pressed him on the ages and his motives, and each time he denied
   anything sexual. So they stopped arguing about intent and turned to what
   the laptop itself could prove: whose computer it was, how often he
   searched, and the number he was about to put on his own collection…
6. Gary confirmed the laptop was his and estimated he had saved thousands of
   pictures. He admitted some involved nudity and blamed that choice on
   drinking. With his computer already in police hands, officers had one more
   thing to ask of him, and one piece of news to deliver…
7. Cooperating didn't change where the night was headed. Gary consented to the
   search and learned he was going to jail. Even then, he offered to delete
   the pictures himself, as if erasing them could still make this go away.
   Before transport, officers drove him home for his medication. But once
   inside, Gary had one last concern, and it wasn't about the pictures…
8. In the end, Gary G. Johnson was arrested and charged with possession of
   "inappropriate files".

Runtime about 19 minutes; blocks between cut-ins ran 2 to 3.5 minutes.

### Reference 3: parents hiding four malnourished children (two-split intro)

First intro clips: three clips, 14 seconds: an officer at the door, then two
lines from inside the house ("we … mess", "oh … fucking …").

1. Albuquerque police officers have just responded to what initially appeared
   to be a routine welfare check. But within minutes of arrival, they'd
   uncover conditions so disturbing that the investigation quickly turned into
   something far more sinister…

Rest of the intro clips: three clips, 15 seconds: the wall inside the house,
officers confronting the parents, the forensic finding.

2. On October 23rd 2025 in Albuquerque, New Mexico, a passerby found a young,
   non-verbal girl wandering alone through the street. Barefoot, wearing a
   heavily soiled diaper, and with no adult in sight. As officers searched for
   where she had come from, what appeared to be a simple welfare check was
   about to uncover something far more disturbing.
3. According to the woman who found her, the young girl had been wandering
   alone down the street before running up and hugging her, repeatedly saying
   she was cold. Unable to identify herself and covered in dirt, she gave
   officers little to work with. Before they could understand why she had been
   left alone, they first had to determine where she had come from...
4. With the young girl safe in a patrol vehicle, officers turned their
   attention to the home she was believed to have wandered from. Although
   dispatch found no prior history at the address, the property's condition
   immediately raised concerns for the child's welfare. After discovering an
   open back gate and rear door, officers prepared to enter the home to
   determine whether anyone else was inside.
5. With no one responding to repeated commands, officers entered the residence
   to conduct a protective sweep. Inside, they found Jonathan Garcia along
   with three more young children, confirming the little girl outside had not
   been alone. As officers began gathering Jonathan's account of what had
   happened, the children's mother unexpectedly arrived at the scene, adding
   another layer to the investigation...
6. With the children identified and Jonathan's account beginning to take
   shape, officers shifted their focus to understanding how the young girl had
   been able to wander away unnoticed.
7. As officers questioned Jonathan about the young girl's injuries and overall
   condition, he attempted to explain away each concern. According to him, the
   cuts on her feet were the result of playing outside, while the soiled
   diaper and his failure to supervise the children stemmed from exhaustion
   after caring for them alone.
8. As officers continued documenting the scene, a medical assessment of the
   young girl revealed injuries that extended well beyond what had been
   observed at the residence. Those findings prompted investigators to request
   an emergency forensic examination, introducing a new and deeply concerning
   aspect to the case...
9. With all four children now being evaluated, officers began documenting a
   pattern of neglect that extended throughout the household. Multiple
   children required medical attention, the home's condition continued to
   deteriorate with each room searched, and drug paraphernalia recovered near
   Jonathan added another critical piece to the investigation. Before long,
   detectives would have enough evidence to begin considering criminal
   charges...
10. In the end, Jonathan Garcia and Vanessa Salazar were both arrested and
    charged with multiple counts of abuse, abandonment, and child exposure to
    methamphetamine.

Runtime about 17 minutes; blocks between cut-ins ran 1.5 to 3 minutes. Two
cut-ins here (6 and 7) end without a tease; prefer the teaser the framework
asks for.
