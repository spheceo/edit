# Writer's house rules

Writer's own rules, from the team's reviews of its bodycam and interrogation scripts. They come after the producer's guides and add to them. Where they are stricter, follow these. (The FOIA and compilation guides are Repack's, kept word for word; these rules live here so the two stay in sync.)

## People
- Introduce every person by full name, and age when the evidence gives it, the first time they matter: "the mother, 27-year-old Rebecca Worthington". After that, use the first or last name, the same one every time.
- A person who matters to the story is named. Never leave the mother, the father or the suspect as "the woman" for a whole case when the footage or a report names them.
- A name first appears in a case opening or story development, never in an outro. An outro only uses names the viewer already knows.

## Places
- Give the city and state, and the agency. Leave out street names, house numbers, hotel room numbers, apartment numbers, school names and businesses unless the story turns on them. Write "a home in Port St. Lucie, Florida", not "a home on Southeast Burgundy Lane"; write "a motel room", not "room 209 of the Red Roof Inn".

## Cut-ins add context; they never summarise
- A cut-in tells the viewer what the footage can't: who someone is, what happened off camera, what changed, what is at stake next. It never retells what the viewer just watched. These are summaries and are wrong: "Reese denied all of it.", "To every question, Benjamin gave the same answer: no…", "Jason blamed Jamilee, and Jamilee blamed Jason…", "Dougie said he would never normally press charges…".
- Each fact is told once in the script. Once the viewer knows he called 911, that she was shot, or where he had gone, don't say it again in a later cut-in.
- Tell the story forward. Never narrate the telling itself: no "To understand how … it helps to go back…", no "Let's rewind". When the footage jumps back in time, give the date and move on: "Earlier that afternoon, …".
- A FOIA cut-in runs 50 to 65 words; a case opening runs no more than 65. The intro keeps its 40 to 55.

## Outros are short and final
- An outro is the case's outcome in one or two sentences, about 45 words at most, opening "In the end, …". It says what happened to the people: arrested, charged, convicted, sentenced, the child safe. Give the furthest outcome the evidence has, without stacking every date, count and the judge.
- Every case's outro has a clear outcome. One that ends on a process ("…to type up the charge against him.") or restates the cut-in before it is wrong.
- Nothing after the outcome: no resignation days later, no follow-up story.
- Keep allegations, charges and outcomes distinct. Never state guilt the record doesn't: not "investigators concluded that he staged it", but "he was charged with…" or "a jury found him guilty of…".

## Care with harm and children
- Tell harm by what it meant, never by the blow: "the baby had been hurt", "she had injuries no fall explains". Never "hit in the head", "punched", "slapped", "kicked" or "choked".
- Never pair a care word with "child" or a child: no "child pornography", "child abuse", "child rape". Write "images of a child", "harming a child", or name the charge once in plain terms.
- Use care words (sexual, rape, abuse, pornography, molest) only as often as the story needs: once to make the charge clear, never in a list of every count.

## Transitions and openings
- Vary how transitions open. No two in a script start with the same words ("Up next, we have a case where…" twice is wrong).
- Every case opening gives the verified date ("On May 29, 2024, …"). When no source gives one, say so to the producer in the report; never leave it out silently, and never guess.
- A case's first block after its case opening runs one to two minutes, so the viewer meets the case before a long stretch.

## Intro clips
- Every intro clip, in the opening and in each case, is one of the strongest moments, 1 to 8 seconds, and it plays again in that case's body, where it happens in the story.
- No filler: an intro clip says something that lands ("Where is she?"), never a stammer ("That's, that, that's,") or a stretch with no line in it.
