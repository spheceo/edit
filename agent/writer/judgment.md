# Judgment calls

These are not rules. Each one is a situation the producer met while working on
a real script, what they chose to do there, and why. The usual shape and the
writing framework stay the default and are right for most cases.

For each one, ask whether this case is actually in that situation. If it is,
weigh the suggestion. If it is not, leave it alone: applying one of these where
its situation is absent makes the script worse, not better. The "not when" line
is as much a part of each item as the suggestion is. Never apply one only
because it is listed here, and never copy an example's wording: the examples
come from other cases.

The hard rules elsewhere (no reading of rights in the intro, careful wording
around children, sourced facts only) are not judgment calls and always hold.

## Choosing footage

### An intro that opens away from the person the title is about

- **When:** the title is about a child, a victim or one particular person, and
  the first intro clips show others instead: officers arriving, witnesses, the
  suspect, the house.
- **Consider:** opening on that person, giving them more than one clip, and
  bringing the others in after them.
- **Because:** it was the producer's most common change to a finished intro,
  in seven different cases: "Have the very first clip be of the daughter, and
  have more clips of her, and then we can maybe have the mother afterwards",
  "boy very first instead", "We need to have more clips of the child in the
  very beginning of the intro", "clips of the girl in the very beginning".
- **Example:** at the bus stop (title: a grandmother beats a six-year-old),
  the intro opened on the witnesses and the grandmother. The producer asked
  for more of the boy, then for the boy first, then the witnesses, then her.
- **Not when:** that person never appears on camera, or the title turns on
  the moment of discovery or a confrontation (officers finding the body, a
  suspect caught on camera), which is then the opening instead.

### Opening clips that are only procedure

- **When:** the quiet opening before the first cut-in would be housekeeping:
  the detective explaining what will happen, paperwork, sorting out a name.
- **Consider:** a moment where they first touch the case, just before the main
  questions begin.
- **Because:** the viewer should be near the case from the first seconds.
- **Example:** in place of the detective explaining the forms: "I'd asked you
  about little David. Is it David or Dave? … And David is your little brother,
  right?"
- **Not when:** the footage has a natural quiet start, such as the detective
  sitting down and introducing themselves. That is still the usual opening.

### The same thing said several times inside a clip

- **When:** a clip holds one question or answer repeated.
- **Consider:** keeping it once: the question and the reply.
- **Example:** "So you're thirteen years old." / "I'm twelve.", without the
  further "thirteen" lines and "That's what I'm saying, twelve".
- **Not when:** the repeat is the point: a suspect changing an answer, or a
  detective asking again because the first answer did not hold.

### An intro clip that runs long

- **When:** one clip in a row of intro clips is much longer than the others,
  such as ten seconds among three-second ones.
- **Consider:** a short line that makes the same point.
- **Because:** intro clips work as quick proof; a long one stalls them.
- **Not when:** the long moment is the strongest thing in the footage and
  cannot be said shorter.

### An intro told in steps

- **When:** each sentence of the intro has a short moment in the footage that
  shows it.
- **Consider:** putting the intro on two or three cut-ins instead of one, each
  followed by one to five short clips that back up the line just read. How many
  cut-ins depends on the case: where a line has no clip of its own, keep it
  with the line before it.
- **Because:** the viewer sees each claim proved straight away.
- **Example:** "This twelve-year-old was left in charge of his younger
  siblings while his mother was out." → "You stay at home with them a lot… but
  you babysit." and "What were the other two tonight when their mom is gone?"
  Then "Now the two-year-old brother in his care lies unconscious in hospital
  with severe head injuries. But the story he tells can't explain what doctors
  are seeing, and the harder the detective presses, the faster it falls apart."
  → five short clips, among them "he didn't get those head injuries just from
  books" and "I don't even know why I pushed them."
- **How to save it:** the first line takes the intro role and the other intro
  lines are saved as bridges. The case opening is then the cut-in after the
  last intro line, whatever purpose read_script prints for it.
- **Not when:** the footage has no clip for the lines. Then the usual shape
  stays: quiet opening, intro, hook, case opening.

### A long stretch of setup after the case opening

- **When:** the first block after the case opening is mostly setup: the full
  reading of rights, who lives where, who shares a room.
- **Consider:** trimming it to what the story needs, then a cut-in early on
  that says what the detective is trying to find out, and carrying on from
  there.
- **Example:** only the first right was kept; then "Tonight you were home… in
  January, he got hurt… how did he break his leg in January?" and a cut-in
  about the January injury, about twenty seconds in, before the conversation
  picked up again.
- **Not when:** the early footage is already tense or revealing. Then it runs
  its usual two to three minutes.

### The date and clock on bodycam footage

- **When:** a cut-in needs the date and the research does not give it ("On a
  Sunday afternoon"), or clips from several cameras or recordings have to be
  put in the order things happened.
- **Consider:** reading the date and clock the camera prints in the corner of
  its picture, usually top right, with inspect_frames, and ordering clips by
  that clock. Day and night light is a quick check on the order.
- **Because:** the producer: "check the top right corner of the footage -
  there you can see the date - august 28, 2022", after three requests for the
  actual date. In another case the clips jumped from a night conversation
  back to daylight: "It's really important that you look at the top-right body
  cam tag to see what the time is."
- **Not when:** the research or the report already gives the date, or the
  footage has no such stamp (interview rooms often show none).

### Cutting a line that something later depends on

- **When:** you are about to cut an exchange.
- **Consider:** checking whether anything kept later refers back to it. If so,
  keep the setup, or cut both.
- **Example:** "like you said, no one travels down that road" needs the
  earlier exchange in which the detective said so.
- **Not when:** the later line stands by itself without the earlier one.

### A clip that comes out of nowhere

- **When:** a clip answers or reacts to something the cut leaves out, so it
  lands with no lead-in.
- **Consider:** bringing in the line just before it that sets it up, and
  taking out the dead air between the two.
- **Because:** a strong line with no setup reads as a jump; the viewer spends
  the moment working out where it came from.
- **Example:** the agent's "…you got a warrant out of Holmes County, Florida
  for accessory to murder" played straight after he ended the interview. The
  producer: "it comes a bit out of the blue if you look at the previous clip."
  With the second detective's "Hey, as long as you didn't know that you got a
  warrant out…" put before it, and the fifty seconds of silence between them
  cut, it landed.
- **Not when:** it is an intro or hook clip, which is meant to arrive without
  context and plays again in its place later.

### A long stretch where nothing is at stake

- **When:** minutes of footage in which the case does not move: a ride in the
  back of the cruiser, talk about belongings, a car or a laptop, release
  conditions read out.
- **Consider:** keeping only the lines the story uses, and letting the rest go
  even when the video runs shorter for it.
- **Because:** the producer called the cruiser footage "pretty low stakes" and
  asked for "significantly less" of it.
- **Example:** in the RV twins case the father's time in the cruiser went from
  about eight minutes to about two. His worry over his things and his car
  stayed, because a cut-in turns on it ("his first request had nothing to do
  with his children"); the rest went. The video went from 18 minutes to 11,
  and the producer kept it.
- **Not when:** the stretch holds an admission, a contradiction or a moment a
  cut-in relies on. A workspace's floor still holds (a FOIA video never runs
  under 10 minutes).

## Writing

### Time words in the intro

- **When:** the intro mentions something that came later: "within months",
  "soon after", "weeks later".
- **Consider:** leaving it for a later cut-in.
- **Because:** the intro is in the present tense, so the viewer hears "within
  months" as months from now.
- **Not when:** the cut-in is in the past tense, where such a phrase is clear.

### Sentences that read as separate facts

- **When:** a paragraph's sentences do not lead into one another.
- **Consider:** carrying one thread through them, so each picks up something
  from the one before.
- **Example:** "left in charge of his younger siblings" → "the two-year-old
  brother in his care" → "the story he tells".
- **Not when:** the paragraph turns on a deliberate break, such as the intro's
  expectation set beside the fact that breaks it.

### A teaser that lists what comes next, or tells it

- **When:** the last sentence reads like a list of what the detective is about
  to ask, or reports what the person goes on to admit later.
- **Consider:** ending on the one open question the next footage answers, and
  holding back the answer and the details a later cut-in carries.
- **Because:** a list or an answer leaves the viewer nothing to watch for.
- **Example, a list:** "The detective wanted him to show exactly how a stretch
  could break a two-year-old's leg, and to say who else was in the room when it
  happened." became "But the stretch he described was one the detective had
  never seen, and soon the questions narrowed to just one: had he done it on
  purpose?"
- **Example, telling it:** a paragraph that went on to "Later in the
  interview, the detective summed up what Cristian had described: he had been
  thinking about his stepfather, got angry, and took it out on David. Now the
  detective wanted every detail, down to how many times David's head hit the
  shelf." became "The questions now centered on a bookshelf in the children's
  room, and on a harder one: why a 12-year-old would take his anger out on his
  two-year-old brother. Then the detective asked him to use a doll to show
  exactly what he had done."
- **Example, too specific:** "But once a clinician and a fire crew examined
  Isabel herself, that version began to slip. One wound on her head didn't fit
  an old fall, and the detective asked to see where she slept…" gave the next
  block away piece by piece. The producer asked for "a more broad teaser", and
  it became "But the moment deputies looked past Laura's words and at Isabel
  herself, a very different picture began to take shape…"
- **Not when:** it is the final cut-in, which closes with the outcome.

### A question whose answer is obvious

- **When:** a teaser ends on a question.
- **Consider:** whether the viewer can already answer it. If they can, tease
  something they cannot know yet, such as the turn the next stretch takes (see
  "A teaser hung on one person's next line" for why that is usually better
  than promising one answer).
- **Because:** the producer: "we shouldn't have fake questions like this if
  they are really obvious."
- **Example:** after the footage showed the check was fake, "…one question
  would decide how serious this was: had that money ever been hers to begin
  with…" became "What counted was how she handled the money once it landed,
  and to the teller, the way she had taken it out looked anything but
  random…" The teller answers that in the next clip.
- **Not when:** the answer is still open at that point in the story: "So if
  she wasn't involved, how did she know what happened to the body?"

### A teaser hung on one person's next line

- **When:** the last sentence promises that one person is about to say
  something: "his answer was blunt", "he still had an answer", "he already had
  a theory", "it is the part they will not let stand".
- **Consider:** teasing the turn the whole next stretch takes, what the scene
  is becoming, and leaving that line to land by itself when it plays.
- **Because:** the producer rewrote one such paragraph five times, then: "make
  a little generic teaser maybe rather than make it focused on him about to
  say one thing only." A teaser built on one line promises more than one clip
  can deliver, and the viewer hears that line already summed up.
- **Example:** "…But when another officer asked what it looked like had
  happened, he still had an answer…" became "…so all anyone had was what the
  detective inside had seen so far. But what little he knew was already enough
  to unsettle everyone on scene, and the full picture of that room was only
  starting to take shape…" Earlier in the same script, "What they found there
  stopped the call cold, and something else in that room made deputies treat
  it as a crime scene…" became "But when deputies stepped inside, what met them
  in that room caught them completely off guard…"
- **Not when:** the line is the turn of the case itself (a confession, a lie
  coming apart) and the viewer cannot guess it.

### A teaser that needs something the cut left out

- **When:** the last sentence turns on a question, a call, an answer or a
  person the viewer has not met in the cut.
- **Consider:** saying in a few words what it is, so the sentence makes sense
  on its own.
- **Example:** "Meanwhile, Laura was on the phone with relatives. When she
  hung up, she had a brand-new answer about who made Isabel's decisions…" The
  producer: "this doesn't make sense on its own." The question had never been
  asked in the cut. It became "Meanwhile, deputies asked who had legal
  authority over Isabel's decisions, and Laura got her cousin Raul on the
  phone. When she hung up, she had a surprising answer…"
- **Not when:** the footage just before shows it.

### Naming someone before the footage reaches them

- **When:** a cut-in names and introduces a person whose own footage comes
  several blocks later.
- **Consider:** calling them by who they are to the case ("her fiancé") until
  then, and giving the name and age in the cut-in where the footage first
  shows them.
- **Because:** a name with no face is one more thing to hold, and it means
  more when it lands on the person.
- **Example:** early in the RV twins case, "The man Madison kept asking about
  was her fiancé, 24-year-old Leroy Somersall III, the father of the twins.
  The same judge had signed a warrant for him too, and he was next." The
  producer: "Let's wait with identifying the fiancé until we have footage of
  him." It became "Madison kept asking for her fiancé, and whether deputies
  were arresting him too…", and the cut-in before his arrest named him:
  "There they found her fiancé, 24-year-old Leroy Somersall III, the twins'
  father."
- **Not when:** the title or the case opening is about them, or the block
  that follows cannot be understood without the name.

### A fact told again after an edit moved it earlier

- **When:** a revision, often the producer's own, puts a fact into an earlier
  paragraph: a name, an age, an amount, an earlier stop.
- **Consider:** reading every later paragraph for that fact and, in the same
  proposal, taking it out wherever it is now told a second time.
- **Because:** the producer had to ask twice: "Reread this accordingly,
  because now we identify her far earlier", then "This is something we
  already know from the very start of the script."
- **Example:** once the case opening read "18-year-old Paris Taylor deposited
  an $8,320 company check… then withdrew $1,003 at one of its branches", the
  third cut-in still began "The young woman in the back of the patrol car was
  18-year-old Paris La'Bertha Taylor of Coral Springs", and the fourth ended
  "…the bank's records showed this wasn't her first stop that day…" They
  became "Paris had come back to the branch expecting to walk out with the
  rest of the money…" and the teller teaser above.
- **Not when:** the later mention adds a new implication; a brief recall is
  allowed then.

### Leaning on the report

- **When:** a cut-in cites the report for something routine, the report comes
  up cut-in after cut-in, or a teaser points at what would end up in a report.
- **Consider:** stating the fact plainly or leaving it out, and teasing the
  moment the viewer will see rather than where it was written down.
- **Because:** the producer: "we don't really want to say 'according to the
  sheriff's office report' in a situation like this. It's more like if there
  is a murder case or something really significant."
- **Example, routine:** "According to the sheriff's office report, his
  behavior got so bad that a patrol deputy was sent to assist." became
  "…until a patrol deputy was called in to help."
- **Example, the teaser:** "Now she demanded to hear the full charge, word
  for word. And what she said when she heard it would end up quoted in that
  detective's report…" The producer: "Not a fan of this. We can do better
  here." It became "Now Madison was about to hear the full charge that
  investigation had produced. And her reply, the moment she heard it, showed
  just how differently she saw her own twins than the deputies holding her
  warrant did…"
- **Not when:** the case is serious and the report is the only source of a
  fact that matters: "according to later reports, the boys had been shut in
  like this for over a year".

### Losing the person the title is about

- **When:** a stretch of footage is taken up by others (a relative's
  outbursts, the pets, a phone call), and the cut-ins follow them there.
- **Consider:** tying each of those cut-ins back to what it means for the
  person the title is about.
- **Because:** the producer: "I feel like we forget what the title is and who
  we try to portray as the victim."
- **Example:** in the case of an 87-year-old woman living with her niece and
  great-nephew, "Jonathon's insults only grew louder, aimed at the deputies,
  their families and their badges, until a patrol deputy was called in to
  help." gained "Not one of his complaints was about the 87-year-old woman
  deputies had come to protect." After the niece said her son beat her, the
  next cut-in opened "If Jonathon could do that to his own mother, what did it
  mean for the 87-year-old aunt living under the same roof?"
- **Not when:** the case has no one harmed at its centre, such as a scam told
  from the bank's side. And keeping the person in view does not mean opening
  every cut-in on their name; see the next item.

### Cut-ins that open the same way

- **When:** several cut-ins close together, or a compilation's outros, begin
  with the same name or words.
- **Consider:** opening each on its own beat: a consequence, a quoted line,
  the other person, the thing at stake.
- **Because:** the producer: "do so not every cut in in the second half of the
  script starts with isabel", and on four outros that began "In the end": "we
  don't just say 'in the end' all the time."
- **Example:** "Isabel turned down the restraining order…", "Isabel blamed the
  marks on animal bites…", "Isabel would not say…" became "The restraining
  order was the one thing that could have kept Laura and Jonathon from walking
  back through Isabel's door, and she turned it down.", "Animal bites and
  scratching, Isabel said…" and "Her warning was clear: Jonathon would beat her
  once deputies left."
- **Not when:** two openings match far apart, or a single-case outro opens "In
  the end", as the references do.

### A paragraph squeezed to save room

- **When:** you are shortening narration to bring the script down to its
  length, and a paragraph is losing the words that join its facts: who was
  involved, why something happened, how one thing led to the next.
- **Consider:** leaving the paragraph whole and finding the room elsewhere: a
  sentence that repeats the footage, a cut-in the story does not need, or
  running a page over and saying so.
- **Because:** the viewer has not seen the case. Facts with the links between
  them taken out stop making sense, however short they are.
- **Example:** squeezed, a case opening read "In June 2006, Bloomington,
  Illinois police got a call about a prowler outside a woman's home. At
  gunpoint, the responding officer recognized the man between the houses:
  41-year-old Sergeant Jeff Pelo. Hours later, a detective asked him to
  explain…" The viewer cannot tell why he was at gunpoint, that he was one of
  their own, or what he was asked to explain. Whole, it read "In June 2006,
  police in Bloomington, Illinois, received a call from a woman who said
  someone was lurking outside her home around 12:30 at night. The officer who
  answered searched the area and found only one man there, standing between
  the houses. When he ordered him to turn around at gunpoint, he recognized
  him: 41-year-old Sergeant Jeff Pelo, one of the department's own. Hours
  later, Pelo sat across from a detective who knew him well, and was asked to
  explain what he had been doing there…"
- **Not when:** the words being cut repeat what the footage is about to show,
  or pile on detail a later cut-in carries. Cutting those is right.

### A line in a narrator's stock voice

- **When:** a sentence sounds like a documentary narrator and tells the viewer
  what they already know or are about to see for themselves.
- **Consider:** putting in its place something specific from the footage or
  the research that the viewer does not know yet.
- **Because:** the viewer can see an interview is being recorded; being told so
  adds nothing, and the stock phrasing sounds borrowed.
- **Example:** "Murray had already talked on the roadside. Now Palmer needed
  him to say it again, on the record…" became "Before Palmer had asked him a
  single question, Murray told him he was the one they were looking for. Close
  to six that morning, Palmer asked him to repeat what he had confessed after
  hearing his rights, this time on camera…"
- **Not when:** the phrase carries its plain meaning and adds a fact. "On the
  record" is fine where someone goes on the record about something the viewer
  did not know.
